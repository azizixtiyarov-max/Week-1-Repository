How will you begin to dream something no machine can dream?

A machine can finish my sentences. It cannot get homesick in a city where the bread tastes wrong. So I start from that homesickness, and I use the machine only to carry back what I find.

If memory is built from shared stories, which dreams do you want written into the past?

I want a past with a table in it that was already set when the new students arrived. In that version, nobody spends three weekends hunting for bread that tastes like home. Someone is waiting with a pot on the stove and a story about the first winter they got through. That is the past I would like to point to, so I write it down now, before anything else exists.

What remains of you when all your stories are rewritten?

The hands, probably. The way you fold dough without counting, the exact pinch of salt, the order you set plates in. Stories get edited and translated until they barely sound like the original, but muscle memory survives a rewrite. I want the kitchen to keep those habits alive, so a dish still tastes like the person who learned it, even after the menu changes.

If you seek to realize your hopes through AI, how will you ensure that it remains faithful to your will?

I keep the machine on the errands and the decisions with people. In my data science courses I have seen how easily a model drifts toward whatever is most common in its data and presents that as the answer. So the AI gathers and sorts: menus, recipes, reviews from students in Xi’an. A person who has actually eaten the food checks every suggestion before it goes on the menu. Every recipe is credited to the person who shared it, and no one’s story is used without a clear yes.

Course one: people and places
  • Which relationships will make your life meaningful and magical? How do you nurture them?

    The ones where someone asks what you ate today and actually waits for the answer. I nurture them with long, unhurried meals and by showing up when nothing is planned.

  • What are the aesthetics of the places you live in, and how did they come to be that way?

    Low tables, warm light, and a wall of handwritten recipe cards stained by years of use. Rooms like that become beautiful because people kept coming back and adding something of their own.

  • How do you spend your time? What stories do you seek out, to tell or to listen to?

    Mostly I listen. I look for the people who remember how things were made, and for classmates whose stories do not fit the usual script.

Course two: sound and motion
  • How will it sound? What will the rhythm of your day be?

    Quiet mornings with code compiling in the background, then a kitchen at dusk with the radio on low. I want a day that moves from the screen to a table, not from one screen to the next.

  • How will you move through the world?

    Slowly enough to notice the markets and the faces, and far enough to bring back what I find. I switch between Uzbek, English, and Chinese, which I am still learning, and I plan to keep switching.

  • How do you spend your time? What stories do you seek out, whether to share or to absorb and pass on?

    I want to pass them on in forms that last: a reading app that teaches Chinese through short articles, and a restaurant in Xi’an where a table keeps the stories going.

The machine can keep every recipe. Deciding which ones are worth dreaming about is still ours.
